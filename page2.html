Mixing vs Mastering: Why Getting the Mix Right Matters More Than You Think

You've written the track, programmed the drums, found the sounds and spent hours tweaking everything until it sounds pretty good. So what's next? Mastering?

Not necessarily.

One of the biggest misconceptions in music production is that mastering is where a track gets its professional sound. Mastering is often seen as the final magic touch that transforms a home-produced track into something that can stand alongside commercial releases.

But mastering isn't magic, and it certainly isn't a substitute for a good mix.
Mixing and mastering are two very different stages of production.

Mixing is where the record comes together. It's about getting all the individual elements working as a cohesive piece of music. Making sure the kick and bass complement each other rather than fight for space, the drums have impact, the synths have room to breathe and everything sits comfortably in the overall arrangement.

It's about balance, dynamics, frequency relationships, stereo positioning, depth and separation. A great mix doesn't simply make everything audible; it makes everything work together.

Mastering is the final stage. It's about refining an already balanced mix, making final adjustments to tonal character and dynamics, managing overall loudness and ensuring the track translates consistently across different playback systems. It's also where consistency and sequencing become important when preparing an EP or album.
The important distinction is that mastering works with the finished stereo mix. It doesn't have independent access to all the individual sounds.
If your kick is overpowering the bass, your percussion is masking the synths, or your midrange is congested, a mastering engineer can make some improvements, but they're working with everything already combined. They can't simply turn down the kick without potentially affecting other elements occupying the same frequencies.

It's a bit like asking a decorator to fix the foundations of a house. They're working at the wrong stage of the process.

One of the biggest differences between an amateur-sounding production and a professional one isn't necessarily the equipment, the plug-ins or even the quality of the sounds. It's balance.

You can have the most expensive synthesiser in the world, but if everything is competing for attention, the result will still sound messy. Equally, a well-balanced production made entirely with stock plug-ins can sound fantastic.

It's about knowing what to listen for, understanding how sounds interact and making decisions that serve the music. Sometimes the biggest improvement comes from removing something rather than adding another processor.

I've been producing and mixing music for over 30 years, working across a broad range of electronic music. Along the way I've worked with artists including Faithless, Tall Paul, Mixmaster Morris and Arthur Baker, and been involved in remixes for Pet Shop Boys, George Michael and INXS.

I've worked through just about every generation of music production technology, from the early days of digital production to today's incredibly powerful DAWs and plug-ins. I've been using Logic Pro since its earliest incarnation and have spent countless hours learning what makes a production translate.

One thing experience teaches you is that great mixes aren't necessarily about having more equipment or complicated processing. They're about making the right decisions.

Modern music production puts incredibly powerful tools into the hands of independent producers. You don't need an expensive studio or a cupboard full of vintage hardware to make a great record. You need an understanding of the tools, a good set of ears and the ability to make everything work together.

Mastering is an important part of the production process, and a good mastering engineer can make a real difference. But the best results happen when both stages are doing the jobs they're supposed to do.

So before spending money mastering your next release, take another look at the mix. It might be where the biggest improvement is waiting.

Get the mix right. Then get it mastered.
